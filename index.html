01 / Frame the problem

The service comes first.

A warm room. A delivered load. A reliable computation. Start with what people need, then find what limits it.

Can sufficiently capable human–AI and AI–AI research systems discover interventions that substantially increase useful energy availability per constrained resource while decreasing total system-level environmental and economic costs?

Or are we merely moving energy, entropy, cost, risk, materials, or environmental damage somewhere less visible?

Same ambition. Different constraints.

These are framing examples, not measured comparisons. Select a service to see what would need to be established.

What do “energy density” and “binding constraint” mean?

Energy density: stored usable energy per litre, or, when stated explicitly, per kilogram. Include the tank, packaging, cooling and other required equipment. A cell figure and a whole-system figure are different comparisons.

Binding constraint: the limit that actually prevents an outcome from improving. Better storage may do little if charging access, price, power or reliability is what limits the service.

Useful service: an outcome with defined quality. A computation must meet a stated accuracy and latency; heat must be delivered at a stated temperature.

Definition context: U.S. Department of Energy, Hydrogen Storage. This source does not establish the project's leverage hypothesis.

03 / Follow the whole system

Where did the entropy go?

And where did the burdens go? A gain inside one device can be offset elsewhere. Entropy is a physical quantity; it is not a catch-all measure of harm.

ENERGY & PHYSICS

What flows in and out?

Usable energy, conversion losses, waste heat and available work. Include the entire system and state its operating conditions.

MATERIALS & ENVIRONMENT

What grows elsewhere?

Extraction, water, land, emissions, toxicity and replacement needs. Report the absolute totals as well as each unit of service.

PEOPLE & DEPLOYMENT

Who carries the cost?

Affordability, safety, access, reliability, supply dependence and construction time. Name affected groups and missing evidence.

Pareto frontier: options for which no feasible comparator is at least as good on every chosen objective and better on one. This only works with comparable boundaries and sufficiently resolved uncertainty. It does not automatically select a preferred option.

04 / Keep both loops in view

More efficient. Less energy?

It depends on total use. Better energy might enable more research—and cheaper computation might increase demand enough to offset an efficiency gain.

POSSIBLE BENEFIT LOOP

Better energy → better research

  1. Better energy
  2. Cheaper / more efficient computation
  3. More scientific computation
  4. Better models, materials & engineering
  5. Potentially better energy ↺
POSSIBLE COUNTER-LOOP

Cheaper computation → more demand

  1. Cheaper computation
  2. More computation
  3. Greater aggregate energy demand
  4. Heat, infrastructure & material demand
  5. Possible loss of the original benefit ↺

A 25% increase in tasks erases a 20% saving per task.

What this illustration leaves out

New energy ÷ baseline energy = (1 − saving per task) × (1 + task growth). It excludes manufacturing, hardware replacement, cooling changes, prices and other sectors. It does not estimate how demand responds to price or establish a lifecycle benefit.

Rebound: extra use caused by an efficiency-related change. Jevons-type backfire: induced use more than offsets savings. Task growth from unrelated causes is not automatically rebound. The benefit loop also requires useful, reproducible discoveries; more compute does not guarantee them.

05 / Investigate, then update

A process that can change its mind.

Independent work first. Evidence exchange next. Converge on a useful next action, while preserving meaningful disagreement.

ExpandChallengeTestConvergeRepeat

Start with several possible problem framings. Seek the cheapest test that could rule one out. Different AI roles do not, by themselves, create independent evidence.

Human and AI roles, independence and safety
  • Explorer: Find competing explanations and neglected interventions.
  • Engineer: Check manufacture, operation, maintenance, deployment and scale.
  • Physicist: Check mechanisms, units, conservation laws and physical limits.
  • Experimentalist: Design the cheapest discriminating test.
  • Systems analyst: Track the service and downstream system changes.
  • Environmental auditor: Find lifecycle burdens and affected communities.
  • Economist: Compare capital, operating costs, access and deployment timing.
  • Red team: Challenge physics, assumptions, metrics, safety, demand and scale.
  • Prior-art investigator: Find patents, papers, abandoned programs and negative results.
  • Synthesis system: Preserve disagreement and identify the next decision.

Freeze the initial question and record each participant's sources, model or expertise, conflicts and exposure to earlier conclusions. Compare independent evidence summaries before sharing conclusions. Preserve dissent and source overlap. This static repository cannot enforce blind review or run a multi-AI system.

Humans are first-class researchers in every role. Hazardous physical work must remain theoretical until qualified professionals complete the appropriate review and authorize its scope.

How evidence and confidence are labelled

Unresolved: the evidence cannot yet settle the bounded claim.

Plausible — experiment required: supporting evidence exists, but the needed test has not resolved the claim.

Falsified: evidence contradicts the registered claim within its stated scope.

Supported within tested conditions: documented independent replication supports the claim within named conditions.

Hypotheses, assumptions, simulations and experimental results are recorded separately. Confidence has a dated rationale; it is never a truth meter.
